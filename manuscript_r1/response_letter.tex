The proposed tool appears to mainly integrate several existing EDM methods without introducing methodological improvements to address known limitations of these models. If the contribution lies primarily in integration, the level of innovation may be limited. In particular, the authors claim that tEDM extends EDM to multivariate and spatially replicated time series, but the manuscript does not describe how this extension is implemented or evaluated in detail. More clarification on the underlying methodological framework is necessary.
\end{point}

The authors should elaborate on why this tool is particularly suited for urban environments and provide supporting analysis or discussion to justify this claim.

The paper does not clearly discuss how the causal discovery results produced by tEDM differ from those obtained using other widely adopted causal discovery algorithms, such as the PC algorithm. \begin{point}
Interpretation of the causal strength derived from the IC curve: I believe that these methods do not so much as quantify the treatment effect of a variable $X_1$, as much as enable to place the strength of this effect in context of other variables $X_2, \dots, X_n$. In a traditional interpretation, the derivation of a causal strength would be the multiplier by how much an increase of $X_1$ alter the outcome $Y$ (modulo confounders, etc.). I am unsure whether the causal strength derived from the AUC of the Cardinality curve can be interpreted this way. SO it may be a good mechanism for causal discovery, or even ranking of the treatment effects of multiple variables, but I am not sure how it would relate to claims about absolute effect strength. Can you address the interpretation of the values you get from your GCMC measure's outcomes?
\end{point}

\begin{reply}
We thank the reviewer for this thoughtful and important comment regarding the interpretation of causal strength. We agree that the notion of causal strength in GCMC differs fundamentally from a traditional treatment-effect interpretation. In the revised manuscript (lines~187--199), we have clarified that the causal strength measured by GCMC is rooted in dynamical systems theory and is defined by the divergence between intersectional cardinality curves under the null and alternative hypotheses of mutual cross mapping. As such, it should not be interpreted as a treatment effect, such as the marginal change in an outcome variable induced by a unit change in a predictor while controlling for confounders.

Instead, the GCMC causal strength represents a relative measure of causal influence, reflecting how consistently the state of one variable can be recovered from another across neighborhood scales in the reconstructed state space. From this perspective, GCMC is particularly well suited for causal discovery and for comparing the relative strength of directional causal coupling among multiple candidate drivers. Larger values indicate stronger causal influence in a comparative sense, enabling ranking and contrast across variables, rather than direct claims about absolute effect magnitudes. We have emphasized that this interpretation is consistent with the empirical dynamic modeling framework, where causation is inferred through information recovery rather than parametric effect sizes.
\end{reply}
