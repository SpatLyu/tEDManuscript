\begin{abstract}
We present tEDM, an R package for discovering causal relationships in urban time series data using temporal empirical dynamic modeling (EDM). This paper introduces the theoretical basis, software structure, and representative applications of tEDM, and highlights its advantages in detecting dynamic and nonlinear causal relationships in complex urban systems.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Urban systems are inherently complex, comprising interdependent socio-economic, environmental, and infrastructural components that interact across multiple spatial and temporal scales \citep{mcphearson2016understand_urban_systems}. These interactions are often nonlinear, adaptive, and subject to feedback loops, making them challenging to analyze with traditional correlation-based approaches and linear regression models \citep{gan2025urban_transfer_entropy}, which typically assume fixed functional relationships, linearity, and stationarity. Such assumptions constrain their capacity to capture the dynamic, nonlinear dependencies and feedback mechanisms that characterize real-world urban processes. As a result, much of the current analytical practice in urban science relies on methods that can identify statistical associations but are not equipped to uncover underlying causal mechanisms.

Causal analysis, in contrast, seeks to move beyond correlation by identifying the drivers and mechanisms that generate observed patterns, thereby providing a stronger basis for effective interventions and informed decision-making \citep{fekete2025urban_transport}. In recent years, it has been successfully applied in urban science to examine the interplay between climate change and human crises \citep{zhang2011climate_change_human_crisis}, assess the impact of air pollution on life expectancy \citep{chen2013air_pollution_life_expectancy}, investigate the effects of ozone exposure on influenza incidence \citep{guo2024ozone_influenza}, uncover the mechanisms underlying the urban heat island phenomenon \citep{zhong2024urban_heat_island}, and explore causality among roads within urban traffic networks \citep{mao2024urban_traffic_networks}. Taken together, these examples provide compelling evidence of the transformative potential of causal inference in uncovering mechanisms not accessible through correlational approaches alone.

Causal inference in quantitative research can be broadly divided into two primary tasks: causal discovery, which seeks to identify the underlying structure of causal relationships among variables \citep{runge2015causal_gateways,runge2019ictsess,runge2023cits}, and causal effect estimation, which focuses on quantifying the magnitude of causal impacts once a causal structure is assumed or identified \citep{pearl1995scm1,pearl2000scm2,rubin1974pom1,rubin1986pom2}. In urban science and related disciplines, causal effect estimation has been widely implemented using quasi-experimental designs such as difference-in-differences (DID), regression discontinuity design (RDD), and instrumental variables, as well as structural equation modeling (SEM) approaches \citep{liu2021quasi_experimental,delgado2015sdid,kolak2019srdd,bowden2015instruments,rosseel2012sem}. These methods have enabled researchers to evaluate the effects of policies, infrastructure investments, and environmental interventions on various urban outcomes \citep{chen2013air_pollution_life_expectancy,jia2021high_speed_rail,li2024protected_areas}. In contrast, causal discovery has received comparatively less attention, despite its potential to uncover hidden causal structures in complex urban systems and to inform the design of more robust and targeted effect estimation strategies \citep{deng2022urban_granger_causality,fekete2025urban_transport,gan2025urban_transfer_entropy}.

Among the existing approaches to causal discovery, one prominent category involves predictive models such as Granger causality \citep{granger1969grangertest,geweke1982grangertest2} and its nonlinear extension, transfer entropy \citep{schreiber2000te1,vicente2010te2}, which have been further adapted to multi-spatial observational and nonstationary urban time series \citep{deng2022urban_granger_causality,gan2025urban_transfer_entropy}. Another influential line of methods centers on graphical causal models, such as the Peter and Clark (PC) algorithm, Fast Causal Inference (FCI), and Peter and Clark Momentary Conditional Independence (PCMCI), which rely on conditional independence testing and structural constraints to infer causal structures \citep{spirtes1993pc,runge2019pcmci,runge2023cits}. A third and increasingly utilized framework is Empirical Dynamic Modeling (EDM), which reconstructs system dynamics from time series data using state-space embedding, enabling causal inference in complex systems without prespecified model structures \citep{lorenz1969ssr,Takens1981embedding,Mane1981embedding,sugihara2012ccm}. By leveraging the intrinsic properties of dynamic systems, such as nonlinearity and feedback, EDM is particularly well suited for capturing causal interactions in urban systems, which are often temporally evolving, multi-scale, and characterized by complex interdependencies. This makes EDM a compelling tool for advancing causal understanding in urban science.

However, despite its conceptual advantages, the application of EDM for causal discovery in urban contexts remains limited. We argue that harnessing EDM in this domain can uncover dynamic causal linkages that are often overlooked by traditional correlation-based or static approaches, thereby providing deeper insights into the mechanisms that govern urban processes. To facilitate such efforts, we introduce \textit{tEDM}, an open-source \textit{R} package that extends the EDM framework with temporal causality detection capabilities tailored to the heterogeneous, high-frequency, multivariate, and multi-spatial observational nature of urban datasets \citep{deng2022urban_granger_causality,gan2025urban_transfer_entropy,fekete2025urban_transport}. Through three representative case studies, we demonstrate how \textit{tEDM} enables more nuanced and accurate causal discovery in urban time series, contributing both methodological innovation and practical value for urban science.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~2 outlines the theoretical foundations of empirical dynamic modeling and its relevance to causal discovery. Section~3 details the design, structure, and core functionalities of the \textit{tEDM} package. In Section~4, we illustrate the practical utility of \textit{tEDM} through three real-world case studies spanning different urban contexts. Finally, Section~5 summarizes the key insights and discusses limitations, as well as directions for future research and development.

\section{Background}

\subsection{Empirical dynamic modeling and dynamic causality}

Traditional causality detection in time series analysis often relies on Granger causality, which assumes that if the historical values of variable $X$ can improve the prediction of variable $Y$, then $X$ is said to Granger-cause $Y$ \citep{granger1969grangertest,geweke1982grangertest2}. However, this approach is commonly implemented using linear vector autoregressive models, whose assumptions of linear interactions and variable separability are often violated in nonlinear and dynamically coupled systems \citep{sugihara2012ccm}. To address these limitations, empirical dynamic modeling (EDM) provides a data-driven, model-free framework capable of capturing nonlinear and inseparable causal relationships directly from time series data. EDM reconstructs the underlying system dynamics through time-delay embedding, enabling robust inference of causal relationships without requiring explicit functional forms or assumptions of separability \citep{Takens1981embedding,Mane1981embedding,sugihara2012ccm,leng2020pcm,tao2023cmc}.

The theoretical foundations of EDM include the generalized embedding theorem and dynamical systems theory. The generalized embedding theorem states that the dynamics of a deterministic system can be reconstructed from time-delayed observations of a single variable \citep{lorenz1969ssr,Takens1981embedding,Mane1981embedding}, producing a shadow manifold in a reconstructed state space that preserves the topological properties of the original system’s attractor. Dynamical systems theory further indicates that if two variables are causally linked, the geometry of their respective shadow manifolds will exhibit a consistent correspondence, allowing states on one manifold to be used to estimate states on the other through mutual cross mapping \citep{sugihara2012ccm,gao2023gccm}. These two processes are jointly illustrated in \autoref{fig_edm}, which depicts both manifold reconstruction and the mapping between shadow manifolds.

\caption{Schematic illustration of empirical dynamic modeling.}
\label{fig_edm}
\end{figure}

Building on this principle, \citet{sugihara2012ccm} proposed convergent cross mapping (CCM) to quantify causality in nonlinear and inseparable systems, with its initial applications focusing on detecting causal relationships in complex ecological systems. Subsequent developments extended CCM to address additional methodological challenges. Partial cross mapping (PCM) \citep{leng2020pcm} was introduced to distinguish direct from indirect causal effects by controlling for potential confounding variables, thereby improving the robustness of causal inference in multivariate settings. Cross mapping cardinality (CMC) \citep{tao2023cmc} further expanded causality detection by shifting the focus from prediction accuracy to the geometric consistency of shadow manifolds, quantified via the intersection cardinality of nearest neighbors.

Beyond methodological refinements, EDM has evolved from an approach limited to time series analysis to one applicable to spatial cross-sectional data \citep{gao2023gccm}. To address CCM's requirement for relatively long time series, alternative models such as cross map smoothness (CMS) and multispatial convergent cross mapping (MultispatialCCM) have been developed for shorter time series \citep{ma2014cms,clark2015multispatialccm}. These advancements have broadened EDM’s applicability across diverse empirical scenarios. Building upon these developments, the \textit{tEDM} package is specifically designed to address the characteristics of urban time series. In particular, it focuses on datasets with prevalent multi-spatial observations and high-dimensional multivariate structures by adapting and integrating CCM, PCM, and CMC with tailored modifications to enhance causal discovery in complex spatiotemporal systems.

\subsubsection{Convergent Cross Mapping (CCM)}

Convergent cross mapping (CCM) tests whether historical states of a response variable $Y$ can reliably estimate the states of a potential driver $X$, based on the assumption that if $X$ causally influences $Y$, then information about $X$ is encoded in the dynamics of $Y$ \citep{sugihara2012ccm}. Let $Y_{(t)}$ and $X_{(t)}$ be the two time series variable, the shadow manifolds can be constructed using:

\begin{equation}\label{eq_ts_embedding}
\begin{split}
M_{x} &= \left[ x(t),\, x(t+\tau),\, x\big(t+2\tau\big),\, \ldots,\, x\big(t+(E - 1)\tau\big) \right] \\
M_{y} &= \left[ y(t),\, y(t+\tau),\, y\big(t+2\tau\big),\, \ldots,\, y\big(t+(E - 1)\tau\big) \right]
\end{split}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\tau$ denotes the time-delay step (lag), $E$ is the embedding dimension, and $M_{x}$ and $M_{y}$ are the reconstructed shadow manifolds of the time series $X$ and $Y$, respectively.

With the reconstructed shadow manifold $M_{y}$, the state of $X$ can be estimated from the state of $Y$ through the cross-mapping procedure:

\begin{equation}\label{eq_cross_mapping}
\hat{X}_t \mid M_y = \sum_{i=1}^k \left( \omega_{ti} \, X_{ti} \mid M_y \right)
\end{equation}

\noindent where $t$ denotes the time point at which the value of $X$ is to be predicted, $\hat{X}_t$ is the predicted value, $k$ is the number of nearest neighbors in the manifold used for prediction, $t_i$ is the time index of a neighbor, and $X_{ti}$ is the observed value of $X$ at time $t_i$. The corresponding weight $\omega_{ti}$ is defined as

\begin{equation}\label{eq_ccm_weight}
\omega_{ti} \mid M_y = \frac{ \mathrm{weight} \left( \psi(M_y, t_i),\, \psi(M_y, t) \right) }{ \sum_{i=1}^{k} \mathrm{weight} \left( \psi(M_y, t_i),\, \psi(M_y, t) \right) }
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\psi(M_y, t_i)$ is the state vector at time $t_i$ in the shadow manifold $M_y$, and $\mathrm{weight}(\cdot,\cdot)$ is a weight function based on the distance metric between two state-space vectors, defined as

\begin{equation}\label{eq_ccm_weight_fun}
\mathrm{weight} \left( \psi(M_y, t_i),\, \psi(M_y, t) \right) =
\exp \left( - \frac{ \mathrm{dis} \left( \psi(M_y, t_i),\, \psi(M_y, t) \right) }{ \mathrm{dis} \left( \psi(M_y, t_1),\, \psi(M_y, t) \right) } \right)
\end{equation}

\noindent Here, $\exp$ denotes the exponential function, and $\mathrm{dis}(\cdot,\cdot)$ represents the average absolute distance between two state vectors in $M_y$, defined as

\begin{equation}\label{eq_ccm_distance}
\mathrm{dis} \left( \psi(M_y, t_i),\, \psi(M_y, t) \right) = \frac{1}{E} \sum_{m=1}^{E} \left| \psi_m(M_y, t_i) - \psi_m(M_y, t) \right|
\end{equation}

\noindent where $E$ is the embedding dimension, and $\psi_m(M_y, t_i)$ denotes the $m$-th coordinate of $\psi(M_y, t_i)$.

The cross-mapping skill $\rho$ is quantified by the Pearson correlation coefficient between the observed and predicted values, with the confidence interval of $\rho$ estimated via the $z$-transformation under the normality assumption:

\begin{equation}\label{eq_ccm_rho}
\rho = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}\left( X, \hat{X} \mid M_y \right)}{ \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X) \, \mathrm{Var}\left( \hat{X} \mid M_y \right) } }
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}\label{eq_ccm_tstat}
t = \rho \, \sqrt{\frac{n - 2}{1 - \rho^2}} 
\end{equation}

\noindent where $n$ is the number of predicted observations, and

\begin{equation}\label{eq_ccm_zstat}
z = \frac{1}{2} \ln \left( \frac{1 + \rho}{1 - \rho} \right)
\end{equation}

In CCM, the prediction skill $\rho$ is evaluated across different library sizes $L$, where $L$ is the number of points used to reconstruct the shadow manifold. Causal influence is inferred when $\rho$ exhibits a positive trend with increasing library size $L$ and shows convergence to a stable, statistically significant value as $L$ approaches its maximum. For the causal direction from $Y$ to $X$, the converged cross-mapping skill is expressed as

\begin{equation}\label{eq_ccm_convergence}
\rho_{x \to y} = \lim_{L \to \infty} \mathrm{cor} \left( X, \hat{X} \mid M_y \right),
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\rho_{x \to y}$ measures the strength of causal influence from $X$ to $Y$ after convergence.

\subsubsection{Partial Cross Mapping (PCM)}
Partial Cross Mapping (PCM) extends CCM by accounting for potential confounding influences from a third variable $Z$. In many geospatial contexts, apparent causality between $X$ and $Y$ may be driven by shared responses to external drivers. PCM controls for such effects by conditioning the cross mapping on $Z$, either through residualization or partial correlations within the reconstructed state spaces. This enables more accurate identification of direct causal links in multivariate spatiotemporal systems.

\subsubsection{Cross Mapping Cardinality (CMC)}
Cross Mapping Cardinality (CMC) evaluates causality based on the structural similarity between the reconstructed attractor manifolds of $X$ and $Y$. It quantifies how frequently nearest neighbors in one manifold correspond to neighbors in the other, under the assumption that causally linked variables share geometric properties in state space. Unlike CCM, which focuses on predictive performance, CMC emphasizes topological consistency, offering an alternative metric for assessing the strength of dynamic coupling in spatiotemporal processes.

\subsection{Open-source toolkits for time series causal discovery}

Causal discovery in time series is a growing area of interest in fields such as econometrics, neuroscience, climate science, and systems biology \citep{runge2019ictsess,runge2023cits}. Numerous open-source toolkits have been developed in both \textit{Python} and \textit{R} to facilitate causal discovery from observational temporal data. These toolkits implement a diverse range of methodologies, including traditional statistical tests such as Granger causality, constraint-based algorithms such as the PC algorithm, score-based and hybrid learning approaches, and more recent techniques derived from empirical dynamic modeling (EDM). Table~\ref{tab_toolkits} summarizes a selection of representative toolkits across these categories. Each package is described by its programming language, core methodological focus, and whether it supports direct application to time series data.

Among these, traditional statistical methods are exemplified by the \textit{lmtest} package, which provides implementations of Granger causality tests based on linear autoregressive models \citep{lmtest}. Constraint-based methods are implemented in \textit{pcalg}, \textit{causal-learn}, and \textit{cdt}, which allow for causal structure learning through conditional independence testing, though they often require additional preprocessing to handle temporal dependencies \citep{pcalg,causal-learn,cdt}. Score-based and hybrid approaches, such as those supported in \textit{bnlearn} and \textit{cdt}, combine structure search with statistical criteria to learn Bayesian networks, but are not inherently designed for time-lagged causality \citep{bnlearn,cdt}. Linear non-Gaussian models, as implemented in \textit{lingam}, assume non-Gaussian noise to identify causal directions and are directly applicable to time series under appropriate conditions \citep{lingam}.

Methods designed specifically for causal discovery in time series include \textit{tigramite}, which employs PCMCI and its variants to robustly detect time-lagged causal relations under high-dimensional and autocorrelated conditions \citep{runge2019pcmci,runge2023cits}. In the context of nonlinear dynamical systems, EDM-based toolkits such as \textit{rEDM}, \textit{pyEDM}, and \textit{crossmapy} implement techniques like convergent cross mapping to infer causality from state-space reconstruction \citep{sugihara2012ccm, tao2023cmc}. Packages such as \textit{multispatialccm} and \textit{fastEDM} further extend EDM capabilities by enabling causal inference on short time series with spatial replication, where spatial replicates are leveraged as surrogates for temporal variation, thereby allowing the application of convergent cross mapping even when the available time series data are too short for traditional EDM approaches.\citep{clark2015multispatialccm,fastEDM}.

To address remaining challenges in flexibility, scalability, and multivariate modeling within the empirical dynamic modeling framework, this work introduces \textit{tEDM}, a novel R package developed for causal discovery in urban time series data. Urban systems often contain short temporal sequences accompanied by multiple spatially replicated observations, along with substantial heterogeneity across spatial locations \citep{gan2025urban_transfer_entropy,mao2024urban_traffic_networks}. \textit{tEDM} extends and refines core methods such as convergent cross mapping, partial cross mapping, and cross mapping cardinality in order to better accommodate these characteristics. Through carefully designed temporal embedding strategies and efficient implementations for large-scale data, \textit{tEDM} enables causal discovery in complex and high dimensional spatiotemporal systems, and serves as a comprehensive and performant extension of existing empirical dynamic modeling toolkits.
